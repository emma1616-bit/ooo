Project 01 · Measuring the invisible

EEG & Brain–Computer Interface Research

Xidian University · 2024.7 – 2025.11 · Student Researcher

It started when I volunteered as an EEG test subject at a campus event. Watching my own brainwaves appear on a screen felt less like an answer than another question — so I emailed the professor and asked to stay. I read across BCI and cognitive neuroscience, then designed a brain–computer synergy training module: individualized attention thresholds mapped onto a “candle-lighting” progress bar, so participants could watch their focus become visible.

Project 02 · Learning research through practice

RIMI Lab Internship & Depression Prediction Research

Univ. of Kansas Medical Center · under Prof. Zhou’s guidance · 2024 – 2026 · research intern & co-author

My interest in medical imaging began when I was eleven, when I first saw Prof. Zhou’s medical images and model outputs on his computer. Years later, beginning in January 2024, I reconnected with him and started working under his guidance through weekly online mentorship.

This experience taught me how research actually works: reading literature by question and method, checking data formats, keeping experiment logs, tracing what each table really supports, and revising claims until they became precise. That process later carried into a concrete project on depression-risk prediction from social-media posts.

In that project, I worked with the eRisk 2017/2018 datasets and helped build symptom prototypes from real user histories, combining learned patterns from online posts with clinical templates for screening. I preprocessed the datasets, tracked the experimental pipeline, and turned the workflow into a figure for the paper. What stayed with me most was the gap between a model’s output and the person behind the data.

lesson 02 — research is careful checking; the hardest part is not just building a model, but remembering the person the model cannot see.

Project 03 · Philosophy of technology

Fluent Errors — who owns a decoded sentence?

predicted by the machine — undertaken by the user ?
Research paper · philosophy / bioethics

Speech neuroprostheses can now produce remarkably fluent sentences. But accuracy leaves a different question unanswered: when is an output actually the user’s communicative act?

I call the troubling case “fluent wrongness”: a plausible sentence is attributed to the user even though they neither intended its meaning nor adopted it. I argue that an utterance is theirs when it faithfully and nonaccidentally expresses their intention, or when they understand and actually adopt content proposed by the system.

I then separate ownership from evidence and repair. Correcting the screen is not enough if listeners or records continue to attribute the error to the user.

I encountered a human problem that mattered to me, and I chose to build something around it.

Founder · Creator

An eating-disorder awareness initiative — told through art and voice

Eating disorders are surrounded by silence and stereotypes. Canvas of Recovery combines my personal history, original illustrations, and treatment-oriented educational content to make the conversation survivable — for others first, honestly, and a little for myself.

The project runs across platforms: illustrated explainers on anorexia and treatment, a podcast where recovery is discussed in first person, and community artwork submitted by listeners.

episodes 01–09

The first nine episodes move from the basics—what eating disorders are and why they happen—to treatment, recovery, family support, and the experiences that are often left out of the conversation.

EP 01Anorexia Is Not “Dieting Gone Too Far”

Anorexia is not simply about wanting to be thin or having too much self-control. I introduce what anorexia nervosa actually is, why it can become medically dangerous, and why I wanted to make this series.

EP 02It Is Never Just One Cause

An eating disorder rarely comes from a single reason. I look at how biology, perfectionism, anxiety, family dynamics, social pressure, and self-image can become tangled together in different ways.

EP 03What Is Happening Inside the Brain?

This episode moves from behavior to biology, exploring research on interoception, reward processing, and neural pathways in eating disorders. I wanted to understand why hunger, fear, body perception, and food can begin to feel so different from what an outside observer expects.

EP 04When Should We Start Paying Attention?

I go through behavioral and emotional warning signs that are easy to dismiss at first. I also briefly explore how future neurotechnology might complement observation, while emphasizing that technology cannot replace clinical judgment.

EP 05Ten Things We Often Get Wrong

“Just eat more” can sound simple from the outside and feel completely different to the person hearing it. This episode unpacks common misconceptions and suggests more thoughtful ways for friends, families, and caregivers to respond.

EP 06What Does Treatment Actually Look Like?

Recovery is usually not one treatment or one turning point. I introduce the roles of medical stabilization, nutrition, psychological support, family involvement, and medication.

EP 07For Families Who Want to Help

This episode is for parents, friends, and family members who may feel frightened and unsure of what to do. I talk about support, conflict, boundaries, harmful comments, and why caregivers also need space to care for themselves.

EP 08Recovery Is More Than Gaining Weight

I think about recovery as gradually getting life back from food, numbers, fear, and the need to be perfect. Relapse can happen, but it does not erase progress.

EP 09The People We Notice Less Often

Eating disorders are not limited to teenage girls. This episode looks at how they may appear differently in men, adolescents, and adults, and why each group can face different barriers to being recognized and supported.

Cooking & Baking

One thing I haven’t mentioned is that I really enjoy cooking and baking.

I like following tutorials for more complicated dishes, adjusting the ratios of different baking ingredients, and sometimes looking into why certain ingredients work the way they do from a chemical perspective — so I can figure out a better combination.

I think that interest comes from my mom. When I was little, she used to make all kinds of dishes and desserts for me, so a lot of my happiest childhood memories are connected with food. As I got older, I started learning to cook and bake by myself.

I really enjoy making something for my family or friends — and seeing them enjoy it.

It’s a simple part of my life, but food has always felt like a way of showing care.

Ways I See

Painting taught me to look longer; calligraphy taught me that precision and interpretation need each other. Both trained the same muscle research uses.

where the looking started

I started drawing as a child, mostly by covering spare paper with whatever came to mind. My family noticed how much I liked it and signed me up for art classes, where I slowly learned the basics of shape, color, and observation. These early pieces are far from polished, but I still like them for the same reason I keep old sketchbooks: they show how long I have enjoyed noticing small things and trying to put them on paper.
